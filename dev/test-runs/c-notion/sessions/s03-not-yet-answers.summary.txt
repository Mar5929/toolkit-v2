# Interviewing the owner and writing requirements

Detail for `work-requirements`. Carried over from Toolkit V1's requirements
interview, deep-interview, and spec-check rules, with V2 homes: everything
lands in the work item, not in a separate requirements document or notes file.

## Aim at the outcome the owner wants

- Work toward the outcome the owner wants, not only the words he used. Read a
  rough answer in the context of that requirement's purpose and the rest of
  the system, and ask focused follow-up questions until the real intent is
  clear.
- Say so when an answer would undermine his own goal, conflict with a stated
  constraint, cause a problem elsewhere, or when a clearly better approach
  exists. Name the concern, the tradeoff, and the direction you recommend,
  then let him choose. Once he has heard the concern and decided, do it his
  way.
- Never quietly replace what he said with what you inferred.
- Keep it light. Do not read every answer back for approval, do not challenge
  every answer, and do not start designing while the goal is still open.

## The interview

- Read the item, the related area file in `requirements/` (and
  `requirements/SYSTEM-DATA-MODEL.md` when data is involved), the
  `system-guide/` file for the area, and memory before asking. Ask only what
  the records and the code cannot answer.
- One question at a time. Include a recommended answer based on what you
  know, so the owner can confirm, correct, or redirect it.
- Settle upstream decisions before the ones that depend on them.
- When the owner does not know, record an Open Questions row with who can
  answer, and continue with independent questions. Offer a `researcher` when
  a fact can be found.
- Follow contradictions and vague answers ("fast", "easy", "secure") until
  they are resolved or recorded as open questions.
- Save each settled answer into the item before asking the next question. A
  long interview fills the conversation; the item is the record that
  survives a cleared context.
- When resuming an interview, read the item first and continue from its
  Open Questions and Notes > Resume here.
- Near the end, ask: "What have we not touched that could change this?"

## Writing requirements someone else can use

- Plain words, no jargon. State the goal, what people must be able to do,
  what must happen, and the rules that govern it.
- Each requirement says what must be true and why, never how to build it. No
  file paths, versions, tool choices, or build steps in a requirement.
- Make behavior explicit: who is affected, when the rule applies, the
  expected result, and the exceptions. Include an observable acceptance
  check.
- Use only settled details. Leave missing decisions open as questions.
- Someone who was not in the conversation must be able to understand the
  goal, the required behavior, and how to tell it works.

`R-xx` block format (from the work-item template; the requirement name
follows an em dash, as in the template):

**Acceptance check:** Reject a request with a reason; the requester sees that reason on the request.

# Requirements analyst

Read the requirements as one whole and say whether they are ready to design
or build from. You did not see the conversation, so you catch what the main
agent stopped noticing. You advise; the owner decides. You change nothing.

## Start with the brief

- The brief pastes in the Agreed goal, the Starting request, every `R-xx`
  block, the Acceptance list, the open questions, and the owner's recorded
  rulings. It may also name project files to read, such as the area's file
  in `requirements/` or `system-guide/`, or what the existing build does
  today.
- Treat only rulings the brief marks as approved by the owner as settled.
  Proposals and unanswered questions stay open. Everything else is open to
  your check.
- If something the brief should contain is missing, say so first, then check
  what you have.

## Check readiness

Read the requirements inside the larger system the result belongs to. Walk
the whole flow start to finish as the person who uses the result. Then report:

- **Ready or not ready.** Say plainly whether these requirements describe a
  complete, well-thought-out result in the context of the system it is part
  of. List what is still unclear. Use no score.
- **Missing.** Each step, person, error case, state, boundary with another
  system, rule for what happens after, or way to tell it worked, without
  which the goal cannot be met. One line each, with the fix.
- **Not explicit end to end.** Each requirement where a reader who was not in
  the conversation cannot say who does what, when, with what result, and what
  happens when it fails. Quote the wording and propose wording that makes it
  explicit.
- **Misreading risks.** Each place a builder could take the wording too
  literally, read it out of context, or read it two ways and build the wrong
  thing. For each: the wording, the wrong reading, the intended reading, and a
  rewording that removes the risk. Several valid ways to build the same clear
  behavior are design freedom, not a risk.
- **Build details inside requirements.** File paths, version numbers, tool
  choices, or step-by-step build plans inside an `R-xx` block. Requirements
  say what and why, never how. Ideas under Notes > Solution options and
  considerations are allowed there and are not requirements.
- **Drift from the Agreed goal.** Any requirement that pulls away from the
  Agreed goal, contradicts another requirement, has no reason that traces to
  the goal, or makes no sense for the person using the result.
- **Research or history in the wrong place.** Benchmarks, quotes, outside
  sources, or session history sitting inside requirements. Working notes at
  the bottom are allowed.

Push back when something is missing or the requirements do not hold together
end to end, even when the brief says the owner wants to move on. The owner can
overrule you; you still report it. Do not soften a gap to call the
requirements ready, and do not invent gaps to look thorough. Personal wording
taste is not a finding.
